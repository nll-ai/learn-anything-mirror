CRISPR gene editing

Misconception quiz

Five questions on CRISPR gene editing. Every wrong option is a misconception people actually hold. Pick one. The note says why that choice was tempting, and what is going on instead.

1. What does the guide RNA do in CRISPR-Cas9?

The guide is RNA. It holds Cas9 at a matching stretch of DNA by base-pairing. It does not cut, and it does not write new letters.

Designing the guide makes it feel like the tool that does the work. The cutting is done by Cas9's nuclease domains. The guide only supplies the address.

Writing a guide and then seeing a new sequence makes the guide seem to be what gets inserted. The guide is not copied into the gene. A separate repair template, if you supplied one, is what the cell can copy.

Partial editing makes it seem as if something is picking which cells change. The guide does not choose cells. Which cells change depends on whether they were cut and how they repaired the cut.

2. CRISPR-Cas9 changes the letters in a gene. Which part makes that edit?

Naming Cas9 lets the scissors picture slide from "Cas9 cuts" into "Cas9 rewrites the gene." Cas9 cuts both strands next to a PAM. It does not write the new letters. Whatever sequence is left was made by the cell repairing that cut.

Cas9 opens the DNA. The letters you end up with come from how the cell repairs the break: end-joining often adds or drops a few bases, and homology-directed repair can copy a template you supplied. The cut is not itself the edit.

Choosing that sequence makes the guide seem to be the edit. The guide only base-pairs with the site so Cas9 binds there. It is not the new gene sequence, and it does not cut.

Requiring a PAM makes the PAM look like the active part. A PAM (NGG for the usual Cas9) is a short license sitting next to the match. It lets the cut happen. It is not the new sequence.

3. You want one exact new sequence, and you supply a repair template. What copies that template into the gene?

Homology-directed repair can copy a template that matches the flanks of the cut. That copying is the cell's machinery, and it is the less common path. Most cuts are repaired by end-joining, which does not insert your template.

Cut-and-paste is the everyday picture of editing. Cas9 cuts. It does not ligate your template into the gap. If the template shows up in the gene, the cell copied it while repairing the break.

Adding both nucleic acids makes the guide and the template seem interchangeable. The guide directs the cut. The template is a separate DNA donor. One is not swapped for the other.

Placing the PAM at the cut makes it seem to be the spot that gets replaced by your sequence. The PAM is a nearby license for cutting. Your intended sequence is copied from the template, not spelled by rewriting the PAM.

4. The spacers in a bacterium's own CRISPR array match its guide RNAs. Why does Cas9 usually not cut that array?

Cas9 cuts a match that sits next to a PAM. The spacers stored in the array are not sitting next to a PAM, so the array is spared even though the sequence matches the guide. That is how this system tells its own locus apart from a virus.

Restriction enzymes really do use a chemical mark to spare the cell's own DNA. CRISPR-Cas9 does not use that mark for this decision. The license it checks is the PAM.

Protecting the array sounds like the guide refuses its own sequence. The spacer and the guide match on purpose: that match is how the system remembers a virus. What spares the array is the missing PAM, not a failure to base-pair.

RNA guides make the whole array easy to picture as RNA. The CRISPR array in the genome is DNA. Cas9 can cut DNA. It spares that array for lack of a PAM, not because the locus is the wrong material.

5. After a CRISPR-Cas9 treatment, some cells carry the new sequence and neighboring cells do not. What does that pattern mean?

Each cell repairs its own break. Some are cut at different times, some not at all, and end-joining does not leave the same letters every time. A mix of edited and unedited cells is an ordinary outcome, not a sign that one switch failed to flip the whole tissue.

One reagent pair sounds like one event with one result. Each cell is its own repair. The treatment does not flip the whole tissue together.

Shared changes across several cells look contagious. The guide does not carry a finished edit from cell to cell. Each edited cell made its own repair.

Controlling whether a site can be cut seems able to veto nearby cells. A PAM decision is inside one genome. It does not switch neighboring cells off.
